1

What Is Data Mining?

An interdisciplinary field, not a single technique.

Imagine you're handed a database with ten years of transaction logs, customer records, and support tickets, and someone asks: "so, what's in here?" There's no query you can write, no single SELECT statement, that answers that question — because you don't yet know what pattern you're looking for. That's the problem data mining exists to solve: turning a large pile of raw, often messy data into knowledge that a human — or another system — can act on. The name is a deliberate metaphor: just as mining extracts a small amount of valuable ore from a large amount of rock, data mining extracts a small number of useful patterns, rules, or predictions from a large volume of otherwise undifferentiated data.

With that motivation in mind, here's the working definition: data mining uses scientific methods, processes, algorithms, and systems to extract knowledge and insight from structured and unstructured data across a broad range of application domains. Notice what that definition is not saying — it isn't one algorithm, one library, or one job title. It's an umbrella term for a whole toolkit of techniques (statistical, algorithmic, computational) applied to the general problem of "find the signal in this data."

Data science is where three fields overlap

If data mining is the activity, data science is the broader discipline that houses it, and it's easiest to understand as three overlapping circles in a Venn diagram — three skill sets that, individually, each get you only partway there:

  • Computer Science ∩ Math & Statistics → Machine Learning. If you can write efficient code and understand the math well enough to implement a learning algorithm, but lack deep context about what the data means in the real world, you land in Machine Learning: you can build a model that fits the numbers, but may not know whether the pattern it found is meaningful or spurious.
  • Math & Statistics ∩ Domain Knowledge → Empirical Research. If you understand the subject matter and the statistical theory, but you're not primarily a software engineer, you land in classic empirical/scientific research — hypothesis-driven analysis that predates modern computing.
  • Computer Science ∩ Domain Knowledge → Software Development. If you understand the business domain and can build working systems, but aren't leaning on statistical modeling, you land in ordinary software development — the corner of this diagram a career software engineer already occupies.
  • All three overlapping → Data Science. The person (or team) who can code, who trusts (or distrusts) a result because they understand the statistics, and who understands the domain well enough to know which questions are worth asking — that's the full skill set. It's rare for one individual to be strong in all three, which is part of why data science is usually practiced by teams.
"Data Scientist (n.): Person who is better at statistics than any software engineer and better at software engineering than any statistician." — Josh Wills

It's funny because it's a backhanded way of saying: nobody is the best at either half — the value is in being competent enough at both to bridge them, plus knowing enough about the domain to point the work in a useful direction.

Where it's applied

Data mining isn't tied to one industry — the same underlying techniques (classification, clustering, anomaly detection, and so on, defined precisely later in this module) get reapplied across wildly different domains. The table below is organized by industry precisely to make that breadth concrete: notice that "detect the unusual case" shows up as fraud detection in finance, intrusion detection in cybersecurity, and disease prediction in healthcare — same underlying task, different label attached to the data.

What changes from project to project is the data you feed them and the domain expertise you bring to interpret the output — not the underlying math.

The Project Lifecycle

Cyclical, not a strict waterfall — every stage routes through a shared data store.

If you've worked on software before, you already have a mental model of a "waterfall" process — requirements, then design, then build, then test, then ship, each phase gated on the last — and you've probably also seen how badly that model fits reality once requirements turn out to be wrong or incomplete. Data mining projects have the same problem, but worse: you often cannot know in advance whether your data even supports the analysis you're planning, because you can't fully understand the data without first exploring it, and you can't finish exploring it without some sense of what problem you're solving. So instead of a straight line, the discipline models its process as a loop — much closer to an agile sprint cycle than to a waterfall SDLC, where you're expected to revisit earlier stages as you learn things downstream.

Problem understanding→ Explore & process→ Modelling & analysis→ Communicate results→ Deploy & operationalize→ Test & monitor ↺ back to the data store

Walking through what each stage actually means:

  • Problem understanding is the requirements-gathering phase — pinning down what question you're actually trying to answer and what a useful answer would look like, before touching any data.
  • Explore & process is the unglamorous majority of real project time: looking at what data you actually have, cleaning it up, handling missing values, and reshaping it into something usable (this becomes its own module next).
  • Modelling & analysis is where you apply the actual algorithms — classification, clustering, regression, etc. — to the processed data.
  • Communicate results means turning model output into something a non-technical stakeholder can understand and act on (the subject of §5).
  • Deploy & operationalize is the equivalent of shipping to production — taking a model that worked in a notebook and wiring it into a live system.
  • Test & monitor doesn't stop once you deploy: models degrade as real-world data drifts away from what they were trained on, so this stage watches for that decay over time.

Crucially, the cycle then loops back to the data store — a shared, central place all six stages read from and write to — rather than terminating. New data arrives, the deployed model's performance changes, and that feeds back into problem understanding for the next iteration. The source material presents this exact same cycle diagram three separate times, unchanged each time — which is itself the point: this loop isn't a one-time onboarding diagram, it's the recurring structure of the entire discipline, worth internalizing early. It's also not mandatory to hit every stage on every project — a small exploratory analysis might stop after "modelling & analysis" and never reach deployment; a mature production system might spend most of its life in "test & monitor," only occasionally looping back further.

Rule

Domain knowledge runs through the whole cycle — it isn't a separate stage, more like a cross-cutting concern (similar to how logging or security touches every layer of an application rather than living in one place). It guides preprocessing decisions (what counts as an outlier vs. a legitimate rare event depends on the field), shapes which modelling choices even make sense, and supplies the context needed to explain a result convincingly at the end. "Always dive into the domain behind the data — it's the key to engineering meaningful features, focusing your analysis, and telling a story your audience understands."

3

Data Representation

To train a model, data must become an informative, numerical representation — and the same signal often has more than one valid encoding.

Here's a fact worth sitting with before going further: every algorithm you'll meet in this bootcamp — every classifier, every clustering method, every neural network — ultimately only understands one kind of input: numbers, arranged as vectors and matrices. It doesn't matter whether your raw data is an email, a photograph, an audio clip, or a molecule; before any algorithm can touch it, someone has to decide how to turn it into numbers without throwing away the information that makes it useful. If you've ever serialized a rich domain object into JSON or a byte stream before sending it over a wire, you've already done a version of this: you picked an encoding that preserves what matters and discards what doesn't. Data representation is that same problem, but the "encoding" now has to preserve statistical or semantic structure, not just object state — and unlike JSON, there is rarely one obvious canonical encoding for a given type of data.

With that framing, here's how the standard data types get represented numerically:

  • Text → TF-IDF. Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency scores each word in a document by multiplying two signals: how often that word appears within this particular document (Term Frequency), and how rare that word is across the whole corpus of documents (Inverse Document Frequency). The intuition: a word like "the" appears constantly in every document and tells you nothing distinctive, so IDF pushes its weight toward zero; a word like "mitochondria" appears rarely across the corpus but, when it does appear, appears meaningfully — so IDF boosts it. The result is a numeric vector per document where locally-frequent-but-globally-rare words dominate — exactly the words a human would consider "topically important."
  • Text → Word embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe). Rather than scoring words relative to a specific corpus, embeddings place every word as a point (a vector of, typically, a few hundred numbers) in a continuous space, positioned so that semantically similar words end up near each other geometrically — "king" and "queen" sit closer together than "king" and "banana." Because this geometric structure is a general property of language rather than of one specific document collection, these embeddings can be pre-trained once on a huge generic corpus and reused across many unrelated projects — much like pulling in a shared library dependency instead of writing the functionality yourself.
  • Images → raw pixel values. Unlike text, an image already is numeric — a grid of pixel intensity values (one per color channel) — so relatively little transformation is needed; the representation decision here is mostly about how that grid gets fed to a model, not whether to convert it to numbers at all.
  • Molecules / graphs → graph representation, or learned node embeddings (Node2Vec). Some data is naturally relational rather than flat — a molecule is a set of atoms connected by bonds, which maps directly onto the graph data structure you already know from computer science: atoms become nodes, bonds become edges. Node2Vec then applies the same idea as word embeddings, but to nodes in a graph instead of words in a sentence — learning a continuous vector per node that captures its structural position and connectivity.
  • Audio → Spectrogram. Raw audio is a waveform — amplitude over time, a one-dimensional signal. A spectrogram transforms that into a visual, two-dimensional representation of frequency intensity over time, often more useful for speech or music analysis because it exposes pitch and timbre information the raw waveform obscures. That said, it isn't the only valid representation — the same signal can also be modeled directly as a 1-D time series, without ever converting it to an image-like form.

The pattern across every one of these examples: there is no single "correct" representation for a given data type. TF-IDF and word embeddings both represent text numerically, but they encode different information and suit different tasks; a spectrogram and a raw waveform both represent audio, but one exposes structure a model might need and the other doesn't. Choosing a representation is an engineering decision, driven by the downstream task and by which structure in the original data you can't afford to lose.

Types of Data & Core Tasks

The vocabulary used to describe every dataset and every problem type in the rest of this bootcamp.

Before you can pick an algorithm, you need a shared vocabulary for describing what you're working with — the same way a software engineer needs to know whether a value is an int or a String before deciding what operations are even legal on it. Data mining's version of that type system operates on two independent axes: what kind of values a column holds (its measurement scale), and how the dataset as a whole is organized (its structure). Get either one wrong and you risk applying an operation that's technically computable but statistically meaningless — like averaging a column of postal codes.

By measurement scale

This axis asks: what can you legitimately do with the values in this column — order them, subtract them, average them? The answer splits first into categorical vs. numerical, and then each of those splits again:

  • Qualitative / Categorical — values that name a category rather than measure a quantity. This splits into Nominal, where categories have no inherent order (gender, hair color — no hair color is "more" than another), and Ordinal, where categories do have a natural order but the gaps between them aren't necessarily equal or numerically meaningful (reviews, grades, status — "good" is more than "poor," but you can't say by exactly how much).
  • Quantitative / Numerical — values that measure an actual quantity, and therefore support arithmetic. This splits into Discrete, values you get by counting (number of students — you can't have 2.5 students), and Continuous, values you get by measuring, which can in principle take any value within a range (height, speed, weight).

Why bother with this taxonomy at all? Because it directly constrains which statistics and which algorithms are valid. You can compute a meaningful average or standard deviation for continuous data; doing the same to a column of nominal categories is nonsensical (there's no "average" hair color), while ordinal data sits in between — you can rank it, but arithmetic on it is shaky. This distinction resurfaces constantly once you start choosing preprocessing steps and models later in this bootcamp.

By structure

The second axis describes the dataset as a whole, not an individual column — essentially, how much of a fixed schema you can rely on. A useful mapping onto things you already know: structured data behaves like a well-defined SQL table, unstructured data behaves like an arbitrary log file or text blob with no guaranteed layout, and semi-structured data behaves like a JSON or XML document — it has keys and nesting you can navigate, but no single schema every record is guaranteed to follow.

TypeDefinitionExamples
StructuredOrganized data adhering to a predefined schemaCustomer tables, transaction records
UnstructuredNo predefined format; harder to processEmails, videos, social posts
Semi-structuredNo relational schema, but has organizational tags/markersJSON, XML, graph data

Core task types

Finally, once you know what your data looks like, you need vocabulary for what question you're asking of it — this table is effectively the menu of question types the rest of this bootcamp will teach you to answer, one module at a time. Broadly, some tasks require historical examples with known outcomes to learn from (classification, regression), while others look for structure with no labeled "right answer" at all (clustering, association analysis) — a distinction (supervised vs. unsupervised learning) later modules make precise.

TaskDescription
ClassificationSorting data into predefined categories (e.g. spam detection)
Association analysisIdentifying relationships between items in large datasets
RegressionPredicting continuous values (e.g. stock prices, recovery time)
ClusteringGrouping data by similarity without predefined labels
OptimizationFinding the best solution within constraints
RecommendationSuggesting items/content based on preferences
Anomaly detectionIdentifying unusual patterns or outliers
PersonalizationTailoring user experiences or interactions
Decision supportInforming decisions via analysis of large datasets
SimulationModelling complex systems to predict behavior
Text miningUnderstanding, mining, or generating human language

Notice that "classification" and "regression" both predict something from labeled examples but differ in what they predict — a category vs. a number — while "clustering" and "association analysis" both look for structure without any labels to guide them. Keeping this vocabulary straight now will save you from misreading later modules, since Module 3 ("Classification") and Module 4 ("Clustering") assume you can already tell these task types apart on sight.

5

Communicating Results & Tooling

A model is only useful once its results are understood — and built with the right tools.

It's tempting, especially coming from a software background, to think the job is done once the model works — the tests pass, the numbers look right, ship it. But a data mining project doesn't actually deliver value at the moment a model produces a correct prediction; it delivers value at the moment a human stakeholder understands and trusts that prediction enough to act on it. A perfectly accurate model that nobody can interpret is, from a business standpoint, roughly as useless as correct code with no way to run it. This is why "communicate results" is its own dedicated stage in the project lifecycle from §2, not an afterthought tacked on at the end.

The three components of storytelling with data

Effective storytelling with data rests on three components working together, and it's worth being explicit about what each one contributes on its own:

  • Data — the underlying evidence itself: the numbers that make the claim true or false.
  • Visualization — charts and dashboards that let a human perceive a pattern faster than they could by reading a table of raw numbers.
  • Narrative — the connective explanation: what happened, why it happened, and what should be done about it now. Data and a chart alone don't tell you what to do; the narrative is what turns an observation into a decision.

Leave any one of the three out and the story breaks down: data without visualization is a wall of numbers nobody will read; visualization without narrative is a pretty chart nobody knows how to act on; narrative without data is just an unsubstantiated opinion. Common chart types worth having in your toolkit: text/big-number, table, scatterplot, line, heatmap/slopegraph, bar (vertical/horizontal/stacked), waterfall, and square-area chart — each suited to a different shape of comparison.

The Python tooling landscape

The last piece of this module is a map of the software ecosystem the rest of the bootcamp will actually use. If you're coming from a career in another language, the closest mental model is a set of well-established, widely-adopted libraries — except in the Python data ecosystem, a small handful of these are so dominant they function as a de facto standard rather than one option among many.

CategoryPython tools
Core / generalNumPy, Pandas, Matplotlib, Seaborn
Machine learningScikit-learn, XGBoost, RAPIDS
Big dataApache Spark (PySpark)
Large language modelsHugging Face Transformers, OpenAI GPT
Deep learningTensorFlow, PyTorch
Hyperparameter optimizationOptuna, Hyperopt, GridSearchCV
Python note — what each tool actually is
  • NumPy — a fast, fixed-type array (ndarray) with vectorized math: array_a + array_b runs the whole elementwise addition in optimized compiled code, instead of an explicit loop like you'd write over an array in Java.
  • Pandas — builds on NumPy for a labeled, tabular DataFrame, conceptually close to an in-memory, mutable ResultSet with a chainable query API (filter, group, join) instead of SQL strings.
  • Matplotlib / Seaborn — charting libraries. Matplotlib is the low-level, highly configurable base; Seaborn sits on top and produces statistically-aware chart types with far less code.
  • Scikit-learn — ready-made implementations of classic algorithms behind one consistent interface: .fit() on training data, then .predict() on new data, whichever algorithm you swap in — the Strategy design pattern, essentially.
  • XGBoost — a single, highly-optimized implementation of one popular algorithm family (gradient-boosted trees), popular enough to warrant its own library. RAPIDS mirrors the Pandas/Scikit-learn APIs but runs on a GPU for speed at large volumes.
  • PySpark — the Python API onto Apache Spark, the distributed big-data engine. Hugging Face Transformers gives you pre-trained language models ready to load; OpenAI GPT means calling a comparable model as a hosted API instead.
  • TensorFlow / PyTorch — the two dominant deep-learning frameworks, both with automatic differentiation (computing gradients for you), so you don't hand-derive the calculus behind backpropagation — a topic that becomes concrete in Module 5.
  • Optuna / Hyperopt / GridSearchCV — automate the search for a model's best hyperparameter settings, instead of hand-tuning by trial and error.

Pre-reads worth refreshing

The remaining modules lean on a handful of mathematical building blocks, worth flagging now so you can identify gaps before they slow you down starting in Module 2: vectors and matrices (multiplication, norms, eigenvalues) are the language data representation (§3) and virtually every model's internals are written in; derivatives and gradients are how models like the ones in Module 5 learn; exponential and log functions show up constantly in probability and in transformations like the sigmoid function; basic statistics (Bayes' theorem, mean, variance, distributions) underlie how models quantify uncertainty; and covariance matrices together with PCA are the standard toolkit for understanding how variables relate and for compressing many correlated variables into a few informative ones. None of these need to be fresh right now — just be ready to lean on them once they reappear.

Test Yourself

Retrieval practice — the single best-evidenced study technique there is.

Cover the answer, try to recall it from memory, then click to check. Getting it wrong and then reading the answer still strengthens the memory — skipping straight to the answer doesn't. Do this before your exam, not while reading for the first time.

According to the Josh Wills quote in this module, how is a "Data Scientist" defined?

"Person who is better at statistics than any software engineer and better at software engineering than any statistician."

In the data-science Venn diagram, which two fields overlap to produce "Machine Learning," and what key ingredient is missing from that overlap?

Computer Science ∩ Math & Statistics. Domain knowledge is missing, so you can build a model that fits the numbers but may not know whether the pattern it found is meaningful or spurious.

List the six stages of the data mining project lifecycle in order. What do all six stages read from and write to?

Problem understanding → Explore & process → Modelling & analysis → Communicate results → Deploy & operationalize → Test & monitor. All six route through a shared, central data store, and the cycle loops back to it rather than terminating.

Per the "Rule" callout in the Project Lifecycle section, why isn't domain knowledge listed as its own separate stage in the pipeline?

Because it's a cross-cutting concern that runs through the entire cycle (like logging or security touching every layer of an application), guiding preprocessing decisions, modelling choices, and how results get explained — rather than living in one place.

In TF-IDF, what two signals are multiplied together to score a word, and what happens to a word like "the" as a result?

Term Frequency (how often the word appears within the document) multiplied by Inverse Document Frequency (how rare it is across the whole corpus). A word like "the" appears in nearly every document, so IDF pushes its weight toward zero.

What is the difference between Nominal and Ordinal categorical data, per the examples given (gender/hair color vs. reviews/grades)?

Nominal categories have no inherent order (e.g. gender, hair color — no hair color is "more" than another). Ordinal categories do have a natural order, but the gaps between them aren't necessarily equal or numerically meaningful (e.g. reviews, grades, status).

What are the three components of effective storytelling with data, and what happens if narrative is left out?

Data, Visualization, and Narrative. Without narrative, visualization is just "a pretty chart nobody knows how to act on" — data and a chart alone don't tell you what to do.

In the Titanic sprint exercise, what age cutoff defined the "child" group, and what were the resulting survival rates for children vs. adults?

Age < 16 defined "child." Children survived at 59% versus 36% for adults, illustrating the "women and children first" pattern that resurfaces later as the running SHAP example in Modules 3 and 7.

Exam Drill

Same skills as Test Yourself, but in the exam's actual answer format.

Real exams rarely ask you to write a paragraph — they ask you to name a technique in a couple of words, reorder scrambled steps, pick every correct item from a list, or fill in one line of code. These drills train that exact shape of answer, not just the underlying concept.

Name it A telecom company wants to group its customers into segments based on usage patterns, with no predefined labels for what the segments should be. In ≤2 words, what task is this?

Clustering. There's no target column to predict — the goal is to discover groups directly from the data's structure, which is exactly what separates clustering from classification/regression (§4).

Select all Which of the following are examples of unstructured data?
  • (a) a column of customer ages
  • (b) a raw audio waveform
  • (c) a table of transaction amounts
  • (d) a free-text product review
  • (e) a JPEG image's pixel grid

(b), (d), (e). Ages and transaction amounts already live in clean rows/columns — that's structured data. Audio, free text, and images have no fixed schema until you deliberately choose a numeric representation for them (§3).

Code Complete this line to compute the mean fare for each passenger class in a Titanic dataframe df: df.____('pclass')['fare'].mean()

df.groupby('pclass')['fare'].mean() — groupby collapses all rows sharing a pclass value into one group before .mean() reduces each group to a single number, the pandas equivalent of SQL's GROUP BY + AVG.

Compute A tiny 2-document corpus: Doc 1 = "red red blue" (3 words), Doc 2 = "blue green green" (3 words). Compute TF-IDF for the word "blue" in Doc 1.

TF("blue", Doc 1) = 1/3. "blue" appears in both documents, so df=2, N=2 → IDF = ln(2/2) = ln(1) = 0. TF-IDF = 1/3 × 0 = 0 — "blue" shows up everywhere, so it does no work distinguishing the two documents (§3).
